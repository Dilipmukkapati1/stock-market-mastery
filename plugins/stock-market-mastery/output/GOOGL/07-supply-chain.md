# Part Two — Deep Research I: The Business in Depth

## The industries, structurally

Alphabet straddles three oligopolies. **Cloud infrastructure** is the cleanest: Synergy Research put Q3 2026's market at $168 billion, growing 55%, with AWS at 26%, Microsoft at 19%, and Google at a record **16%** — a tight three-firm structure (CR3 = 61%) in which Google is the share-gainer (13% in Q2 2025 → 15% → 16%). **Digital advertising** is a three-firm structure with a definitional trap: EMARKETER's April 2026 forecast, on a *net* revenue basis, has **Meta passing Google in 2026** ($243.46B vs $239.54B; shares 26.8% vs 26.4%, Amazon 9.0%, top three = 62.3%) — while Alphabet's *reported* advertising revenue is gross of TAC and far larger. Both statements are true; the definition chooses the winner, and the net basis is the economically honest one: about a fifth of what advertisers pay Google leaves immediately as distribution cost. **Search** remains the extreme case: 89.94% worldwide share (StatCounter, September 2026), structurally protected by defaults — the quality of the protection, not the product, is what two U.S. antitrust cases have been litigating.

## The model and the moat, as hypotheses

The flywheel (Part One) is the model; the moat claims deserve the discipline of hypotheses with evidence:

*Distribution and defaults.* Claim: default placement plus habit keeps search share near 90% regardless of rival quality. Evidence for: a decade of share stability against free one-click rivals; the remedies ruling left payments legal. Evidence against: the same ruling mandates data sharing and bans exclusivity, and AI interfaces reset habits — AI Mode's growth shows users *will* change how they search when the product changes. Status: intact, with its first real crack (regulatory, not competitive).

*Full-stack cost advantage.* Claim: owning the model, the silicon, and the fleet gives Alphabet structurally lower AI unit costs. Evidence for: Cloud's operating margin expanding to 36.2% while growing 103%; TPU cost analyses (Part One, Product Quality) showing cost-per-token at or below GPU rivals. Evidence against: the advantage is bounded to Google-shaped workloads, its magnitude is estimated by third parties rather than disclosed, and it is purchased with $200B-a-year capex whose depreciation is only beginning to land. Status: plausible, partially evidenced, unproven at the return-on-capital level — the level that counts.

*Data and scale.* Claim: the largest query, video, and maps corpora compound model quality. Evidence for: Gemini's frontier standing arrived alongside the industry's largest usage surface (32 billion tokens/minute across products and APIs, per the Q3 call). Evidence against: frontier-model leadership has rotated among labs with far smaller data estates; synthetic data and reasoning techniques keep devaluing raw corpus advantages. Status: a real but depreciating asset.

## The financial story, 2025–2026

FY2025 (10-K): revenue $402.836B (+15%), Cloud $58.705B, capex $91.45B, free cash flow $73.27B. That was the last year of the old regime. Through 2026 the capex guide escalated in steps — $175–185B (February), $180–190B (Q1), $195–205B (Q2, held in Q3) — quarterly capex reached $51.355B, free cash flow turned negative for two consecutive quarters, the buyback stopped, and the company became a net *issuer* of equity ($12.4B ATM in Q3) and debt ($28.7B of notes in Q3; long-term debt $125.1B). The income statement compounded the vertigo with the equity-marks line: +$99.0B of paper gains in Q2 (EPS $9.11 vs ≈$2.85 underlying), −$8.664B in Q3 (EPS $2.12 vs ≈$2.67 underlying). Read through the noise, the operating trajectory is: revenue growth accelerating to +25%, operating income +30%, Cloud passing $30B a quarter — funded by converting the strongest balance sheet in technology into the largest build-out in technology. The debate is not about whether the operations are excellent. It is about the price of the conversion.

## The latest earnings event, dissected (Q3 2026)

*Against the company's own guidance:* the one hard guide — 2026 capex $195–205B — was held exactly; the promised 2027 color was given verbally ("increase significantly," detail on the Q4 call). *Against consensus:* revenue $127.577B vs ~$127.9B expected (a slight miss); EPS $2.12 vs ~$3.02 consensus, mostly the un-modeled SpaceX mark, with underlying ≈$2.67 still short of that consensus. *Drivers in dollars:* Cloud added roughly $15.5B of year-over-year revenue (to $30.709B, +103%); Search added ~$6.8B (+12%, decelerating from Q2's +17% on the tougher comparisons management had flagged); subscriptions added steady growth; Network was flat. *One-time register:* the −$8.664B SpaceX mark (non-cash, below the operating line); the $5.2B EC fine paid (cash, inside the $42.290B operating cash flow — a recurring *category* of cost even when each fine is "one-time"); and the working-capital build for TPU systems, which management explained flows backlog → inventory → revenue through 2027. *The verdict:* the stock fell **12.64% on October 29 — its worst day on record** — and another 1.4% on October 30, erasing roughly $550 billion of value, while the analyst cascade (Goldman $435→$385, BofA $430→$380, JPMorgan $425→$375, RBC $425→$400, Cantor $420→$350, DA Davidson to $300 Neutral; Guggenheim and Needham reaffirming $450 Buys; average target ~$408 across 58 analysts, as compiled from dated notes) mostly *kept Buy ratings and cut targets*. That pattern is the tell: the fundamental story survived; the multiple didn't. The market repriced free cash flow, dilution, and the discount on distant returns — not demand. Any reading of this quarter that stops at "EPS missed" has missed the quarter.
