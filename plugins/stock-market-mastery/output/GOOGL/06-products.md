# Part One — Customers

Alphabet has three customer classes, and confusing them produces most analytical errors about the company: the advertisers who pay, the enterprises who contract, and the users who mostly don't pay at all — but whose attention is the inventory.

**Advertisers — the paying core.** Millions of advertisers buy through auctions; no single advertiser is remotely material, and Alphabet does not disclose customer concentration because there is none to disclose. The advertising relationship's fragility is not concentration but *dependence on distribution*: the same quarter's $82.012B of advertising revenue required $16.366B of TAC payments to partners — Apple above all — who sell Alphabet its place as the default. Alphabet's true "key customer" problem is inverted: it is the dependent party in its most important commercial relationship, and the 2025 search-remedies outcome (exclusivity banned, payments allowed, data shared) repriced that dependence rather than removing it. Part Two covers the regulatory position in full.

**Enterprises — the contracted growth.** Cloud customers range from startups to the largest AI labs, and two facts define the class in 2026. First, visibility: the **$561 billion backlog** (up from $514B in Q2), with over 55% expected to convert within 24 months, gives Alphabet contracted demand most companies cannot match. Second, the customer list now includes the AI industry itself: Anthropic (up to 1 million TPUs, multi-gigawatt commitments from 2026–27), Apple, Oracle — and roughly **100 TPU customers** in total per Kurian on the Q3 call, from hyperscalers to the Blackstone-backed Crux AI venture that will operate TPU capacity as a business. These are deep, multi-year, infrastructure-grade relationships with formidable switching costs — and a new wrinkle: several of Alphabet's largest cloud customers (Anthropic, OpenAI via GCP's NVIDIA capacity) are also its most dangerous competitors in models. Alphabet sells picks to the miners competing with its own mine, deliberately.

**Users — the inventory and the moat.** Roughly: Search's billions of users, YouTube's audience (the largest in U.S. television), the Gemini app's 1 billion users, AI Overviews' 2.5 billion, Android's ~3 billion devices. Users pay with attention and data rather than money — except the growing subscriber class inside the $14.290B subscriptions line (YouTube Premium/TV, Google One, Play), which is quietly becoming a real consumer business. The user's switching cost is habit plus defaults; the entire antitrust saga of Part Two is, at bottom, a fight over how durable that inertia should be allowed to remain.

**Concentration verdict.** Revenue concentration: low (advertisers atomized, cloud diversified, backlog broad). Dependency concentration: real but specific — Apple as distribution gatekeeper on one side, and a small set of AI-lab mega-customers whose TPU and cloud commitments now move Alphabet's capex plan on the other. Alphabet's customer risk in 2026 is not losing a customer; it is that its largest customers' demand is what obliges the $200 billion build-out — if their demand pauses, the commitments remain (Part Two: $924.4B of purchase obligations).
