# Part One — Management

Alphabet is founder-controlled, professionally run, and currently engaged in the largest capital-allocation pivot in its history — from returning cash to raising it. Understanding who decides, and how their incentives point, explains most of what the rest of this book describes.

**The founders decide.** Alphabet's dual-class structure gives Class B shares ten votes each. The 2026 proxy (DEF 14A, record date April 6, 2026) reports Larry Page at 27.4% and Sergey Brin at 25.3% of total voting power — **52.7% combined**. Public shareholders own most of the economics and a minority of the control: the 2026 decisions investors debated most — capex approaching $200 billion, zero buybacks, equity issuance — were never subject to a shareholder veto. Management evaluation at Alphabet therefore means evaluating the founders' judgment as much as the executives' execution.

**The operating team.** Sundar Pichai (CEO of Google and Alphabet) runs a deliberately layered structure: Thomas Kurian built Google Cloud from a distant third into a $30-billion-a-quarter business at a 36% operating margin; CFO Anat Ashkenazi, in seat since 2024, is the voice of the new financial regime — precise guidance, honestly framed (it was Ashkenazi who explained on the Q3 call that TPU system sales flow through backlog, then inventory, before revenue, and who pre-flagged the margin drag from third-party capacity). In August 2026 the AI leadership was reshuffled — Demis Hassabis moved to Chair of Google DeepMind and Alphabet Chief Scientist, Koray Kavukcuoglu to SVP of Google DeepMind — while Jeff Dean and Sanjay Ghemawat departed to co-found a startup (as reported by secondary sources). The pattern to note: Alphabet's bench is deep enough to absorb senior departures that would destabilize most companies, and its AI leadership transitions have so far been promotions-from-strength rather than crisis moves.

**Capital allocation: the record and the reversal.** For a decade Alphabet was a model allocator: buybacks in the tens of billions annually ($11.5B in Q3 2025 alone), a fortress balance sheet, disciplined M&A (the $29.5B Wiz acquisition, closed March 11, 2026, being the large exception that fits the cloud-security strategy). In 2026 the machine reversed. Buybacks have been **$0 all year**. In Q3 alone the company issued $12.4 billion of stock through an at-the-market program plus $28.7 billion of notes; long-term debt roughly doubled year over year to $125.1 billion. The cause is not distress — operating cash flow was $42.3 billion in Q3 — it is the AI build-out's scale (Financials, Part One). Management's defense, made explicitly on earnings calls, is contracted demand: a $561 billion cloud backlog. Whether issuing equity at these prices to fund data centers was wise is the central capital-allocation question of the book, and it is genuinely open: the same management that compounded value for a decade is now asking shareholders to fund the largest infrastructure program in corporate history on the strength of AI demand curves.

**Credibility ledger.** What management said vs what happened in 2026: the capex guide was raised twice ($175–185B → $180–190B → $195–205B) and then **held exactly** in Q3 — guides at Alphabet are ceilings-and-floors, not aspirations. Cloud growth promises were exceeded (82% in Q2, 103% in Q3). The flagged Search deceleration arrived as flagged (tougher comparisons, delivered honestly). Against that: the 2027 capex answer ("increase significantly," detail on the Q4 call) is a deliberate deferral the market punished, and the buyback suspension was discovered in the cash-flow statement more than it was announced. Net assessment: an operationally credible team whose communication lags its strategy by about one quarter — a gap that cost shareholders roughly $550 billion of market value in two days in October (Part Two).
