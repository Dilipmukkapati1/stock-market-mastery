# Chapter 8 — Reading Private-Company Numbers Like a Skeptic

**Learning objective:** Build a repeatable method for parsing any pre-IPO company's claimed figures — the document-literacy skill for a world without 10-Ks.

## Technique 1: The three-bucket sort

**Plain English.** Every number goes in one bucket: (1) **company-disclosed** — the company said it (blog, founder interview); real but self-selected; (2) **press-reported** — a journalist wrote it, usually from insider sources; credible but unaudited and sometimes wrong; (3) **analyst-estimated / modeled** — someone's calculation (Sacra's ARR estimate, NPM's $44.15 model price); useful but assumption-laden. Never let a bucket-3 number do a bucket-1 job.

**Worked example — Glean's price per share.** "$44.15/share" is bucket 3 (NPM model estimate). The "$260.80 bid / $188.50 ask" figures on the same page are bucket "unverified display" — share-class basis unexplained, unusable. Neither is a trade.

## Technique 2: Kill the zombie numbers

**Plain English.** Bad figures shamble on through aggregators long after debunking. This research caught one: a low-quality site claiming a "$150M Series F in early 2026" — contradicting all primary reporting that Series F closed June 2025. Rule: when one source contradicts five independent ones, the one is wrong until proven otherwise. Check dates before citing anything.

**DIY exercise.** Take any funding announcement you read this week and check the round date against two other sources. You will be surprised how often they disagree.

## Technique 3: ARR vs. revenue vs. run rate

**Plain English.** ARR (annualized recurring revenue) is a snapshot of *contracted* subscription value — higher quality than a run rate, which annualizes one good month of *any* revenue. Glean's "$300M ARR" is bucket 2 but structurally strong: contracted, recurring, seat-based. Still ask: over what customer count, at what churn? (~200 customers, churn undisclosed.)

## Technique 4: What "no data" tells you

**Plain English.** Glean has disclosed no audited financials, no 409A, no net revenue retention, no gross margins, no churn, no customer-concentration percentages. Each absence is information: it tells you what the company chooses not to compete on. List the missing numbers before you list the claimed ones — the gaps are the diligence agenda.
