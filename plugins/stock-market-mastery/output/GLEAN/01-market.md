# Chapter 1 — How Pre-IPO Equity Works

**Learning objective:** Explain, in plain terms, what it means to own (or buy) shares of a company that has never gone public — and why it behaves nothing like a stock ticker.

## Concept 1: Private shares are contracts, not tickers

**Plain English.** A public stock trades thousands of times a day, so there is always a price. A private share is a contract between you and the company: you can only sell when the company (or an approved buyer) lets you, and at a price you negotiate. No ticker, no daily quote, no "sell" button.

**Formal definition.** Pre-IPO equity = shares of a privately held corporation, transfer-restricted by the company's charter, investor agreements, and securities law (typically Rule 144 / Regulation D in the US), with no public market for price discovery.

**Why it matters for Glean.** Any headline "valuation" you see — $7.2B — is the price of one negotiated financing round (Series F, June 2025), not a market price. You cannot look it up and sell into it tomorrow.

**Common mistake.** Treating a funding-round valuation like a market cap. A market cap is thousands of trades a day agreeing on a price; a round valuation is one investor, on one day, agreeing with the company.

**DIY check.** Ask: "If I needed my money back next month, who exactly would buy these shares from me?"

## Concept 2: Preferred shares vs. common shares (and the 409A)

**Plain English.** Investors in funding rounds buy *preferred* shares — they get paid first if the company is sold, and they negotiated the price. Employees get *common* shares (or options on them), priced by an independent appraisal called a **409A valuation**, which is almost always lower than the preferred price — typically 40–70% of it at growth stage (reference range; varies with risk).

**Formal definition.** Preferred stock carries liquidation preference and protective provisions; common stock is residual. The 409A is a third-party fair-market-value appraisal of common stock used to set employee option strike prices for tax compliance.

**Common mistake.** Assuming employee-share prices on secondary platforms equal the headline valuation. They are usually priced off the common/409A level, not the preferred round price.

**DIY check.** For any pre-IPO offer, ask: "Am I buying preferred or common, and what was the last 409A?"

## Concept 3: Secondary markets and tender offers — the Glean case

**Plain English.** Private shares change hands three ways: (1) **company-approved secondary sales**, (2) **tender offers** (the company or a new investor buys employee shares at a set price), and (3) **secondary platforms** (Forge Global, Nasdaq Private Market, EquityZen, UpMarket) that broker transfers — usually at a 10–30% discount to the last round price (reference range for late-stage).

**Glean specifics.** Nasdaq Private Market shows an **estimated price of $44.15/share (Oct 2, 2026)** — explicitly a *model estimate* from market activity and public valuation data, not a trade price. The same page displays bid/ask/last-trade figures whose share-class basis is unexplained — treat as displayed-but-unverified, not usable. No confirmed Glean tender offer, secondary trade price, or public 409A figure has been found.

> **Source:** Nasdaq Private Market · Glean company page · Oct 2026 · https://www.nasdaqprivatemarket.com/company/glean/ (model estimate, not a transaction)

**Common mistake.** Seeing "$44.15" on a secondary platform and concluding that's the tradeable price. A model estimate is a guess with good inputs — not a bid you can hit.

**DIY check.** Before trusting a platform price, ask: "Is this a completed trade, an asking price, a model estimate, or just the last round's mark?"

## Concept 4: Lockups and the IPO path

**Plain English.** When a company finally IPOs, early shareholders usually can't sell for a **lockup period** — typically 90–180 days after listing (reference range). The "IPO pop" you read about accrues to people who were already holding, and only if they can sell after the lockup at a price above their entry.

**Why it matters for Glean.** No IPO has been announced; outside commentary guesses "two to three years," which is commentary, not guidance. There is no timeline to anchor any liquidity expectation.

**Common mistake.** Buying pre-IPO shares assuming an IPO is 12–18 months away. Many "pre-IPO" companies stay private for a decade; some never list.

**DIY check.** Ask: "What is my exit if this company never IPOs?" (Answer paths: acquisition — note Moveworks and Sana both exited via M&A — tender offer, secondary sale, or nothing.)
