# Part One — The Company

Intel is two companies stapled together: a product business that still earns real money, and a manufacturing business — Intel Foundry — whose losses currently consume much of it, and on whose conversion into a merchant business the entire equity story rests.

| Q3 2026 | Revenue | Operating income |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Client (CCPG) | $8.097B (−5%) | $2.285B |
| Data Center & AI (DCAI) | $7.318B (+78%) | $3.039B |
| Intel Foundry | $6.697B (+58%) | −$1.853B |
| All Other | $0.692B (−30%) | $0.179B |
| **Total (after eliminations)** | **$16.513B (+21%)** | **$2.522B** |

**The product company.** Client computing — PC processors, renamed CCPG in 2026 reporting — is the legacy cash engine: $32.2B of FY2025 revenue at strong margins, now in a cyclical downturn (Q4 2026 guided down over 20% sequentially). Data Center & AI sells Xeon server CPUs and, nominally, AI accelerators; Q3's +78% growth — the best in fifteen years, at a 41.5% segment operating margin — is the AI build-out's host-CPU attach arriving at exactly the moment Intel finally had a competitive part (Xeon 6, Granite Rapids). "All Other" holds the remnants of the old conglomerate after the portfolio edits: Altera's 51% sale to Silver Lake (closed September 2025) and the decision to keep and fold in the networking/edge unit (December 2025).

**The manufacturing company.** Intel Foundry's $6.697B of Q3 segment revenue is nearly all *intersegment* — Intel's product groups buying wafers from Intel's fabs, eliminated in consolidation (−$6.291B of eliminations). External revenue — actual merchant customers — was **$411M in Q3, and the 10-Q notes it arose mostly from one non-recurring contract**. Against FY2025's baseline (Foundry revenue $17.8B, operating loss −$10.3B, external revenue $307M for the whole year), the picture is exact: a very large internal manufacturer with a very small merchant business attached. The strategy — "IDM 2.0" — is to keep the integration (design and manufacture under one roof, using TSMC selectively where it wins sockets) while converting the fabs into a business that outsiders pay to use, anchored by advanced packaging (EMIB/Foveros) where Intel's position is genuinely strong.

**How it makes money.** Products earn when Intel's chips win sockets in PCs and servers against AMD and, increasingly, Arm-based rivals. Foundry will earn when — and only when — external customers commit volume to Intel's process nodes (18A today, 18A-P next, 14A conditionally) and packaging. Until then the fabs are a fixed-cost engine whose output the product groups must absorb: in Q3 the product groups earned $5.3 billion of operating profit, and Foundry spent $1.85 billion of it. That staple — profitable products funding an unproven manufacturer — is the company. Everything in Part Two is about whether the staple holds long enough for the conversion to complete.
