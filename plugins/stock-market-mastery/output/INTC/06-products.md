# Part One — Customers

Intel's customer story splits cleanly in two: the product customers it has served for decades — and the foundry customers it does not yet have, whose absence is the central fact of the equity story.

**Client customers: the PC ecosystem.** Intel sells processors to the PC OEMs (the global laptop and desktop makers and their ODM supply chain) — a concentrated buyer set purchasing in enormous volume on annual platform cycles. The relationship's economics: Intel's roadmap *is* the OEMs' product calendar, which makes design wins (Panther Lake's breadth; Nova Lake's expected doubling) the leading indicator of this business. The current condition of the class is cyclical pain, guided by Intel itself: client revenue down over 20% sequentially in Q4 2026, with the slowdown expected to last at least through H1 2027 (CFO Zinsner). No single OEM is disclosed as a concentration risk; the class risk is the market, not a buyer.

**Data-center customers: hyperscalers and enterprises.** Xeon sells to the cloud hyperscalers, server OEMs, and enterprises — buyers who qualify platforms over quarters and split sockets between Intel, AMD, and increasingly their own Arm designs. Named, checkable relationships in 2026 include **Oracle**, planning OCI X14 systems around Diamond Rapids in a multiyear arrangement — the kind of forward commitment that evidences platform confidence. The class's defining dynamic: the largest buyers are also building Intel's substitutes (AWS Graviton-class Arm CPUs and their peers), so every hyperscaler socket is simultaneously a sale and a negotiation against the customer's own silicon program.

**Foundry customers: the empty chair.** This is the chapter the whole book leans on, and its content is short. External foundry revenue was **$411M in Q3 2026 — and the 10-Q states it arose mostly from one non-recurring contract** (Q1: $174M; Q2: $293M). The named candidates are all, as of October 30, 2026, something less than customers: **Tesla's "Terafab"** — Elon Musk's April 22, 2026 statement of intent to build 14A capacity with Intel — is a stated plan, *not* a binding contract, per SpaceX's own SEC correspondence (and TSMC was in parallel talks in October). **Apple on 18A-P** is a reported preliminary arrangement (WSJ, May 8, 2026) confirmed by neither party. Microsoft and Amazon appear in earlier, vaguely-scoped foundry agreements whose status is undisclosed. Management has wired the consequence into strategy: 14A proceeds only with a significant external customer signed. Until a name signs a volume commitment, Intel Foundry's customer chapter reads: one non-recurring contract, a plan, a rumor, and a gate.

**The state as stakeholder-customer.** The U.S. government is not a revenue customer of consequence, but it is the demand-side's political guarantor — CHIPS funding, the equity stake, and procurement gravity that make "the American option" a real line in every customer's Intel-vs-TSMC deliberation. Whether that gravity converts to signatures is, again, the open question — the one BofA named when it downgraded the stock the day after Q3 (Part Two).
