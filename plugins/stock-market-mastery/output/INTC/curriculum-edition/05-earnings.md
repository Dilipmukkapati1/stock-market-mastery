# Layer 5: Earnings — Intel (INTC)

> Teaching document · not investment advice · generated 2026-10-31

## Learning objective

After this layer you can dissect Intel's Q3 2026 print the professional way: a genuine operational beat, a guidance miss that mattered more, the one-time items on both sides, and the analyst-revision cascade that turned a strong quarter into a −28.58% day.

## Why this layer matters for INTC

Intel's October 29, 2026 report is the mirror image of Alphabet's (whose strong print also crashed): Intel *beat* the quarter and *cut* the future. Turnaround stocks trade on trajectory, and this layer teaches you to grade the trajectory — guidance, segment momentum, and management's own words — rather than the headline beat.

## Concepts

### Concept 1 — Beat the quarter, miss the future

**Anchor question:** How can a company beat expectations and fall 28.58% in a day?

**Plain English:** The reported quarter is history the moment it prints; the price is a claim on the future. Guidance is the company's own forecast — the single most powerful line in any release. Intel's Q3 beat was real; its Q4 guide (revenue $15–16B vs ~$17.1B consensus, EPS $0.10 GAAP vs ~$0.43) told the market the near future was worse than modeled, and the future won.

**Formal definition:** Beat/miss vs guidance applies to the *outlook* as much as the past quarter: guide-vs-consensus gap = (guided midpoint − consensus) ÷ consensus. For Q4 revenue: ($15.5B − $17.1B) ÷ $17.1B ≈ **−9.4%** — a thesis-sized miss.

**Formula:** Reaction decomposition: price change ≈ f(past-quarter surprise, guidance surprise, strategic news). At Intel in 2026 the guidance term dominates — the stock is a trajectory claim (Layers 1 and 3 explain why).

**Reference range:** Guide-downs of 5–10% on revenue at large-caps typically cost 10–25% of market value when the stock embeds a recovery; Intel's −28.58% sits at the severe end because the guide also pushed the recovery *timeline* out (PC weakness "at least through H1 2027").

**Worked example (INTC):** The beat, for the record: Q3 revenue $16.513B (+21% YoY), DCAI +78% (best in 15 years), gross margin 43.0/44.1% — ahead of the company's own prior outlook on the strength of data-center demand. Then the guide: Q4 revenue $15–16B, gross margin 37.1/38.0%, EPS $0.10/$0.25, with client revenue guided **down more than 20% sequentially** and two named drags — the PC slowdown and the Fab 28 incident (~$300M revenue, ~200bp of margin). The stock fell from $92.07 to $65.76 the next day. Lesson formalized: at a turnaround, *the guide is the earnings*.

> **Source:** Intel Q3 2026 earnings release · 2026-10-29 (results, guidance, incident impact); consensus figures from pre-print previews in research notes (labeled).

**Common mistake:** Headline-scanning "Intel beats!" from a wire summary written off the first table. The market reads the guidance table. So should you.

**DIY check:** For any print, write the guide-vs-consensus gap for revenue and EPS before reading any commentary. Predict the direction of the stock move from the gap alone; check yourself against the actual move.

### Concept 2 — Drivers: one engine racing, one stalling

**Anchor question:** Which segments produced the beat, and which produced the guide-down?

**Plain English:** A blended +21% hid a split company: data center riding the AI build-out's CPU attach (every GPU rack needs host CPUs), client PCs rolling into a downturn as the post-pandemic and AI-PC waves digest. Driver analysis assigns each surprise to its segment and its cycle.

**Formal definition:** Driver decomposition: revenue change = Σ segment changes. Assess each segment against (a) its own cycle position and (b) what the print implies for the next four quarters, not just the last one.

**Formula:** Q3 YoY revenue bridge (approximate, from segment tables): DCAI +$3.2B-ish of the growth (from ~$4.1B to $7.318B), Foundry segment +$2.4B-ish (mostly intersegment — eliminations remind you it isn't external demand), CCPG −$0.4B-ish. Consolidated +21% is mostly one segment's story plus internal accounting volume.

**Reference range:** When >100% of a company's growth comes from one segment, the print is a segment story; grade that segment's durability (here: AI-server CPU attach rates and Granite Rapids supply) as the whole thesis.

**Worked example (INTC):** The call's texture matched the tables: management described Xeon (Granite Rapids) demand as *tight* — supply-constrained, the good kind of problem — while the CFO (Zinsner) framed the client downturn as a multi-quarter digestion ("at least through H1 2027"). Two cycles, opposite signs, one stock: the market capitalized the longer-lasting one (the client/guide story reaches into 2027; the DCAI surge was already in the price after a +78% YTD run). Reading drivers means asking "how long does each driver persist?" — persistence, weighted by size, is valuation.

> **Source:** Intel Q3 2026 earnings release · 2026-10-29; earnings call commentary as reported in research notes (company statements, labeled).

**Common mistake:** Annualizing the racing segment and ignoring the stalling one. Blended growth rates are where nuance goes to die.

**DIY check:** Build the segment bridge for Q3 from the release's segment table. Mark each segment: tailwind or headwind into Q4, with the guide's own numbers as evidence.

### Concept 3 — One-time items, both directions (again)

**Anchor question:** What in Intel's Q3 GAAP result should not be extrapolated?

**Plain English:** Intel's Q3 GAAP net income of $3.320B was flattered by items that will not repeat on schedule: equity gains, a large interest-and-other credit, and a small escrow mark gain. The discipline from the GOOGL book applies unchanged: build the register, sign everything, then judge the operating core ($2.522B GAAP operating income; 19.6% non-GAAP margin).

**Formal definition:** One-time register = the itemized list of non-recurring or non-operating gains/losses with size, line, cash character, and treatment. Intel conveniently discloses most of its register in the release itself.

**Formula:** Q3 register (as reported): **+$330M** equity gains; **+$1,130M** interest and other, net (credit); **+$120M** escrow derivative MTM gain; restructuring charges were small in-quarter ($54M). Sum of the three gains ≈ $1.58B ≈ nearly half of GAAP net income — the register *is* the story of the GAAP number.

**Reference range:** If identified one-time gains exceed ~25% of net income, quote the operating lines and the register together — never the bottom line alone.

**Worked example (INTC):** Symmetric honesty test: in Q3 the register *helps* GAAP (gains), while the nine-month register *destroys* it (the −$12.614B escrow marks, Layer 4). An analyst who adjusts only the nine-month figure but celebrates Q3's $0.60 EPS is doing marketing, not analysis. The defensible through-line: Intel's Q3 *operations* earned a 15.3% GAAP operating margin (19.6% non-GAAP) on strong DCAI mix — good, improving, and entirely separable from the register noise in both directions.

> **Source:** Intel Q3 2026 earnings release · 2026-10-29 (other income components, restructuring); Form 10-Q Q3 2026 · filed 2026-10-30.

**Common mistake:** "Adjusted" numbers supplied by the company adopted uncritically — or rejected uncritically. The register method trusts *items*, not adjustments: each item earns its treatment.

**DIY check:** Rebuild Q3's register from the release. For each item write: repeat? cash? operating? Your three answers determine its treatment — compare with Intel's non-GAAP bridge.

### Concept 4 — The revision cascade: reading the Street's verdict

**Anchor question:** What do 17 target cuts in a day actually tell you?

**Plain English:** After a thesis-moving print, analysts revise models and targets in a cascade. The *pattern* of revisions — who downgrades vs who holds, and what reason each cites — is data: it reveals which assumption broke. For Intel on October 30, the cited break was consistent: not Q3, not even Q4 alone, but the absence of foundry customer traction against a pushed-out timeline.

**Formal definition:** Estimate revision = change in an analyst's forward EPS/revenue model after new information. A downgrade (rating cut) signals a thesis change; a target cut with rating held signals a multiple/timeline change.

**Formula:** (Reading rule) Cluster the stated reasons. If ≥⅔ of revisions cite the same assumption, that assumption *was* the stock's price.

**Reference range:** Post-print target dispersion is information: Intel's post-crash targets spanned ~$70–$100 — a 40%+ spread that quantifies genuine uncertainty, not sloppiness.

**Worked example (INTC):** The October 30 cascade (research notes, opinions labeled): **BofA (Arya) downgraded Buy → Neutral, $145 → $100**, citing "not enough traction with external foundry customers" and pushing EPS power out to 2030; **Roth** Buy → Neutral ($120 → $70); **Jefferies** ($120 → $85); **Baird** ($125 → $83); **DA Davidson** ($100 → $70). Contrast: JPMorgan had *upgraded* to Neutral ($85) on October 19 — pre-print — on Tesla/foundry execution hopes; Citi called it a "strong beat, weak outlook." The cluster is unambiguous: the market's Intel question is the foundry customer question (Layers 2, 3, 7), and Q3 did not answer it. When the same assumption drives both the run-up and the crash, you have found the thesis's load-bearing wall.

> **Source:** Analyst actions of 2026-10-30 compiled in research notes · (analyst opinions, dated and labeled; not facts about Intel).

**Common mistake:** Averaging targets into false comfort ("average $85, so upside!"). Dispersion and stated reasons are the information; the average is the least useful number on the page.

**DIY check:** After any big print, collect five revisions and cluster their stated reasons in one line each. The most-cited assumption is your monitoring priority for the next quarter.

## Common misconceptions

1. "Intel beat, so the crash was irrational." — The price fell on guidance and timeline, both rational inputs at a trajectory stock.
2. "Analyst targets are predictions to average." — They are dated opinions; their reasons and dispersion are the data.
3. "One-time gains make the quarter great." — Register them, then judge operations: 15.3% GAAP operating margin is the quarter's honest core.
