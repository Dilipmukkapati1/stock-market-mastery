## Concepts

### Concept 1 — Market cap and the "large but not mega" tier

**Anchor question:** How big is Intel now, and what does its tier imply for who owns it and how it trades?

**Plain English:** Market cap (price × shares) sets the tier. Intel at ~$349 billion is a large-cap — big enough for every index and institution, small enough (unlike the $3T+ mega-caps) that a single narrative shift can reprice it by tens of billions in a day.

**Formal definition:** Market cap = share price × shares outstanding. Large-cap is conventionally ~$10B–$200B+, mega-cap above ~$200B–$1T depending on the era's convention; Intel sits at the boundary — index heavyweight, but not index-dominating.

**Formula:** Oct 30, 2026: $65.76 × 5,310M shares ≈ **$349B**. Check against the $92.07 prior close: ≈ $489B — one day erased ~$140B of market value.

**Reference range:** A one-day move of ±25%+ is routine for small-caps, rare for large-caps, and almost unheard-of above $1T. Frequency of extreme moves rises as cap tier falls and as event-risk (turnarounds, binaries) rises.

**Worked example (INTC):** On October 30, 2026 INTC fell **−28.58%** ($92.07 → $65.76) on its Q4 guidance (Layer 5). The S&P 500 the same day fell 1.82% — Intel's fall was a stock event, not a market event. Yet even after the crash, the stock was up ~78% year-to-date (Finnhub calculation) because it had entered October priced for a foundry triumph. Tier lesson: at ~$350–500B, Intel is large enough to matter to indices and small enough to be repriced like a speculation.

**Common mistake:** Assuming large market cap means low volatility. Volatility follows *uncertainty about the story*, not size — turnarounds are volatile at any cap.

**DIY check:** Compute Intel's market cap at its 52-week high ($142.35) and low ($32.89). The spread — roughly $430B of opinion — is the turnaround debate measured in dollars.

### Concept 2 — When P/E breaks: multiples on broken earnings

**Anchor question:** What do you do when a company's trailing earnings are negative or meaningless?

**Plain English:** The P/E ratio divides price by earnings per share. When EPS is negative — or polluted by one-time accounting items — the ratio returns nonsense, and professionals switch tools: price-to-sales, forward estimates (with humility), or sum-of-the-parts reasoning.

**Formal definition:** Trailing P/E = price ÷ trailing-twelve-month diluted EPS (GAAP). It is *not meaningful* ("n/m") when TTM EPS ≤ 0 or is dominated by non-recurring items. Price/sales = market cap ÷ TTM revenue.

**Formula:** INTC price/sales at Oct 30: $349B ÷ ~$56B TTM revenue (book arithmetic from FY2025 $52.9B plus 2026 quarters) ≈ **~6×** — versus a historical Intel range nearer 2–3× sales in its decline years and ~6–7× at prior cycle peaks (approximate teaching ranges).

**Reference range:** Mature chip companies commonly trade at ~2–6× sales depending on cycle position and growth; paying peak multiples requires believing the recovery, which is precisely the open question here.

**Worked example (INTC):** Intel's TTM GAAP EPS is negative — not because operations lost money in Q3 (GAAP net income was +$3.320B) but because the nine-month result carries **$12.614B of non-cash mark-to-market losses on the U.S. government's escrow shares** (Layer 4 explains the mechanism). Any data screen showing "INTC P/E: negative" is telling you about an accounting structure, not about valuation. The honest sentence: Intel's valuation currently rests on 2027–2028 expectations, not trailing results.

> **Source:** Intel Q3 2026 earnings release · 2026-10-29 (nine-month results, escrow mark-to-market as reported); research notes on valuation data as of 2026-10-30.

**Common mistake:** Screening out every "negative P/E" stock — or worse, treating a tiny positive P/E built on one-time gains as "cheap." Read the EPS before dividing by it.

**DIY check:** Find INTC's TTM EPS on any data site and then find the escrow item in the 10-Q. Explain in one sentence why the first number cannot be used.

### Concept 3 — The rate backdrop and long-duration equities

**Anchor question:** Why does a chip turnaround care about the federal funds rate?

**Plain English:** Intel's value story is mostly *future* cash flows (foundry profits in 2028+, a restored product cycle). Future money is discounted at today's rates; higher-for-longer rates shrink the present value of distant turnarounds more than of near-term earners. Rates are the tide under every long-duration story.

**Formal definition:** Present value PV = Σ CF_t ÷ (1 + r)^t. "Duration" in equities: the weighted-average time until cash flows arrive. Turnarounds and pre-profit growth have high equity duration.

**Formula:** A cash flow 5 years out at r = 4% is worth 82 cents on the dollar today; at r = 6%, 75 cents. The *ratio* of values (0.75/0.82 ≈ 0.91) is the repricing a 2-point rate rise implies for that cash flow alone.

**Reference range:** Federal funds target 3.75–4.00% (held October 28, 2026, with two dissents *for a hike* — an unusually hawkish hold). The S&P 500 stood +10.73% YTD at 7,580.07 on October 30, 2026 — a bull market that nonetheless contains Intel's crash (dispersion again).

**Worked example (INTC):** Intel's October path shows the interaction: the stock ran to $142.35 (52-week high) on foundry-hope flows in a market paying up for AI-adjacent duration, then gave back a quarter of its value in a day when the *timing* of the payoff slipped (Q4 guide, PC weakness into H1 2027). When a thesis is "profits in 2028," both rate rises and schedule slips hit the same place: the present value of distant money.

> **Source:** Federal Reserve FOMC press release · 2026-10-28; S&P 500 and INTC price data · research data files retrieved 2026-10-31.

**Common mistake:** Thinking rates only matter to companies with big debt. Intel has $50.1B of debt (Layer 4) — but the bigger channel is the discount rate on its story.

**DIY check:** Take any analyst's 2028 EPS estimate for Intel you can find. Discount it crudely at 4% vs 7% over two years and compare. That gap is rate risk in equity form.

### Concept 4 — Event risk and position reality: what kind of holding is this?

**Anchor question:** How should a learner think about a stock that can move ±25% on a single print?

**Plain English:** Some stocks are compounders you check yearly; some are event trades wearing a blue-chip costume. Intel in 2026 is the second: quarterly prints, roadmap milestones (18A yields, a named foundry customer), and policy headlines each carry double-digit repricing power. Owning it through an index fund dilutes that risk; owning it directly concentrates it.

**Formal definition:** Event risk = exposure of price to discrete, scheduled or unscheduled information releases (earnings, product launches, regulatory decisions), as distinct from continuous market risk. Realized volatility = the annualized standard deviation of returns actually experienced.

**Formula:** (Teaching relation) Single-stock risk contribution ≈ position weight × stock volatility. At the index weight (~0.5%-ish for INTC in the S&P 500 — approximate), even a −28% day costs an index holder ~0.15%.

**Reference range:** Mature mega-caps typically move 3–7% on earnings. Intel's recent earnings-day moves have been multiples of that — the signature of a binary-narrative stock.

**Worked example (INTC):** October 2026 alone: the stock was upgraded by JPMorgan on October 19 (to Neutral, $85 target — pre-print optimism on Tesla/foundry execution), then fell 28.58% on October 30 after guidance, triggering **17 target cuts in a day** (research notes). An investor who met Intel only through an index fund experienced a rounding error; a concentrated holder experienced a year of returns repriced in an afternoon. Same company, same news — utterly different risk, determined entirely by the vehicle and weight (the GOOGL book's Layer 1 makes the same point from the index side).

> **Source:** Analyst actions compiled in research notes · October 2026 (opinions, labeled); price history · research data files.

**Common mistake:** Judging a stock's risk by its brand or its past. Intel was a Dow-component sleep-well stock for decades; the risk is what the *current* distribution of outcomes is, not what the logo used to mean.

**DIY check:** Look up INTC's moves on its last four earnings days. Average the absolute values. Compare with the S&P 500's typical earnings-season day. That ratio is event risk, measured.

## Common misconceptions

1. "Big company, safe stock." — Intel repriced by ~$140B in a day; size dampens, it does not protect.
2. "Negative P/E means worthless." — Here it means the EPS input is broken by escrow accounting; fix the input, then judge.
3. "A stock up 78% YTD must be low-risk." — Momentum and fragility coexist; the run-up *was* the setup for the fall.
