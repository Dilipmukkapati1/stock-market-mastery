# Part One · Section 2 — The Company

NVIDIA designs the computing platform the AI industry runs on. It is a **fabless** company: it designs chips and systems, and partners (above all TSMC) manufacture them. What it sells has escalated in scope for a decade — from a chip, to a card, to a rack, to a whole data-center design it calls an "AI factory."

The business today, by the fiscal 2026 numbers (year ended January 25, 2026; company filings):

- **Data Center: $193.7B of $215.9B revenue (about 90%)**, up 68%. Inside it: $162.4B of compute (GPUs and CPUs) and $31.4B of networking — the NVLink, InfiniBand and Spectrum-X fabric that lets tens of thousands of GPUs act as one computer. Networking grew 142% in fiscal 2026; the interconnect is becoming a second business.
- **Gaming: $16.0B**, up 41% — GeForce RTX cards, the original business, now a single-digit share of revenue.
- **Professional Visualization: $3.2B**, up 70% (workstations, Omniverse).
- **Automotive & Robotics: $2.3B**, up 39% (DRIVE platform, robotics).

From fiscal 2027 the company reports two platforms instead: **Data Center** and **Edge Computing** (gaming, professional visualization, automotive and robotics move into the latter). The message is explicit: NVIDIA is a data-center infrastructure company with an edge-computing side business.

The moat is the stack, not the silicon alone. CUDA — the programming platform with millions of developers and two decades of libraries — means a customer's models, tooling and engineering habits are built on NVIDIA's software. Around it sit the full system: GPUs, the Vera/Grace CPUs, NVLink, ConnectX and BlueField networking, and software like cuDNN, TensorRT and NVIDIA AI Enterprise. A rival chip must beat not a chip but an installed civilization. Part Two tests how much of that moat the hyperscalers' own chips can route around.

The one-line summary: NVIDIA converted a graphics company into the toll booth of the AI buildout — and the toll, at a 75% gross margin, is very high.
